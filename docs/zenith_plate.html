Zenith Plate

The building shape that sheds the most heat by thermal radiation is a broad, gently cambered plate that faces the cold zenith. Round 2 adds what turns that plate into a year-round building: deep still-air cells, a programme sunk under an earth vault, night-flush stacks, and a deep pit that stores winter sky-cold for summer.

Round 2 · verdict

The sky makes the cold, the ground keeps it

I sorted every candidate by its physical role before testing it. A sink is something colder than the 16–21 °C water loop. A store moves cold through time. A barrier cuts heat gains. Transport moves heat to a sink. The ranking that came out is simple. The sky is the only sink that works at all three sites in every month. A deep pit is the only way to use winter's large sky surplus in summer. Insulation and mass decide how big that pit must be.

Evaporation contributed nothing over the year at any of the three sites. That is not because it is weak: on a dry June day a wetted plate rejects 4.3 kWh m⁻². It fails because the sky window and the wet-bulb are both governed by water vapour. Where evaporation works, the sky already works, and the two fail in the same humid months. Only storage breaks that link.

4×

Floor area per plate. With an 8,000 m³ seasonal pit, the 3,456 m² plate carries four sunk storeys (8,640 m²) at the hot-dry site with no backup cooling. Round 1's design met 78 % of the load of a single storey. The humid site needs 12,500 m³, or 20,000 m³ if the pit also dehumidifies. The arid site needs no pit (CAE-11).

+43 %

From still air, not aerogel. A 100 mm film-covered still-air cell gives 0.72 kWh m⁻² per day at 20 °C water (dry design day), against 0.50 for 26 mm. A 6 mm aerogel cover gives 0.27 (CAE-8).

22 %

A pit insulates itself by size. A 20,000 m³ pit gains 22 % of its 8 K capacity from the soil each year. A 1,000 m³ pit gains 116 %. Soil at 10 m sits at 21–26 °C, so the ground is a store, never a sink (CAE-10).

−56 %

Night flushing cuts energy, not the peak. Stack ventilation removes 56 % of the annual load at the hot-dry site, all of it outside summer. On the summer design day night air never drops below 23 °C, so it removes nothing (CAE-9, 11).

The list you asked for matches the Persian yakhchāl almost item for item: a thick domed store, a deep pit, shallow pools cooled at night by the sky and by evaporation, and walls shading the pools from the sun. The models here reach the same division of labour. The sky (with evaporation in dry winters) makes the cold, the pit and dome keep it, and shading protects the source. The difference is that this design stores water at 12–20 °C instead of ice.

Round 1 · verdict

Shape the envelope like the sky, not like a radiator

Radiation does not care how much surface a building has. It cares about how much projected area the envelope shows to each direction, weighted by how cold the sky is in that direction. The sky is cold only in a cone around the zenith, in the 8–13 µm atmospheric window. So the optimal envelope is a horizontal, plot-filling emitter, and everything that looks sideways or down should be an infrared mirror.

I started with a second hypothesis: that angle-selective mirror cavities in the skin would beat a flat emitter. The ray tracing falsified that for buildings. Spectral selectivity beats angular selectivity once mirror losses and the extra absorbed sun are counted. The optimiser opened the cavities to about 85°, where they turn into the rib grid that holds the wind-screen.

−76 kW

A 60 m tower gains heat. At 8 K below ambient, a tower with the same volume and skin as the plate is a net heat gainer. Its walls see warm ground and horizon. A slab of the same volume rejects +177 kW (CAE-2).

0.44

kWh m⁻² per day at 20 °C water (hot-dry) for the final skin. That is 4.6× an open PDRC roof (0.096) and 7× a white roof (0.06) (CAE-6).

−3 %

Cost of the structural camber. A rise/span of 0.04 costs 0.2–2.5 % of night emission, and it buys shell stiffness and drainage (CAE-7).

10 K

The plate's whole temperature budget. Flux falls to zero about 10 K below the water temperature (q/hᵣ). Conduction to the emitter has to cost well under 1 K, so channels sit at 150 mm in 1.5 mm aluminium (CAE-4).

Hypothesis · reasoning

The governing identity

A black convex body emits Aₚ(ω)·L into each direction ω, where Aₚ is its projected area seen from ω. Its faces cannot see each other, so its net exchange with the surroundings is exactly:

P = ∫4π Aₚ(ω) · ΔL(ω) dω
ΔL(θ) = Σbands εb [ Bb(Ts) − (1 − τb(θ)) Bb(Ta) ] , τW(θ) = τ₀1/cos θ (8–13 µm), τNW = 0

So the envelope is a projected-area filter laid over the sky's coldness pattern ΔL. Three consequences follow, and the CAE below tests each one.

H1 · Massing

Put projected area where ΔL is largest and positive, near the zenith. No convex shape beats the plot area seen from the zenith. Walls and soffits see θ > θ₀, where the sky turns into a heat source. Prediction: low, broad, horizontal massing, with sideways surfaces made low-ε.

Confirmed · CAE-1, 2, 7

H2 · Skin micro-geometry

Mirror-walled cavities with acceptance θₐ ≈ θ₀ can shape Aₚ(ω) in a way no convex body can. They pass the zenith cone and reflect the horizon. Prediction: a funnel skin outperforms a flat emitter.

Only deep sub-ambient, broadband Falsified at system level · CAE-3, 6

H3 · Scale freedom

Geometric optics has no length scale above λ ≈ 10 µm. Cell size can therefore be set by film span, convection suppression and fabrication rather than by optics: 300 mm cells, 26 mm ribs. The macro scale (camber) can serve structure.

Used in design · CAE-4, 5

CAE-1 · sky model

The sky is cold only overhead

Two-band clear-sky model. The window transmits τ₀1/cos θ, and the rest of the IR spectrum is opaque at air temperature. Hemispherical sky emissivity comes out at . The useful cone narrows quickly as the surface is pulled below ambient, and much faster in humid air.

Coldness pattern π·ΔL(θ), hot-dry

W m⁻² per π sr. Negative means the sky in that direction heats the surface.

Useful-sky half-angle θ₀

Zenith angle where ΔL crosses zero. Humid air at −15 K leaves an 11° cone.

Flat-face net flux vs. tilt

0° = roof, 90° = wall, 180° = soffit. At −10 K a wall gives −24 W m⁻², against +36 for a roof.

CAE-2 · massing

Eight envelopes, one volume, one skin

Each shape encloses 24,000 m³ and wears the same PDRC skin (ε 0.96/0.90, αsol 0.04). The faces of each convex hull were integrated hourly over the design day against sky, ground and sun. Two operating modes are shown: a sub-ambient sink at ΔT = −8 K, which is a building cooling itself, and above-ambient rejection at +15 K, which is process heat or a data hall.

Per unit of skin and of land

Above ambient the tower reaches 1,636 W per m² of land, but it needs 36 % more skin per watt than the low dome. Below ambient the land figure is set by the zenith projection, which is the plot.

CAE-3 · skin optics, Monte-Carlo ray tracing

Testing the angle-selective cavity

A vectorised 3-D ray tracer was written for this study. It models square inverted-pyramid funnels and East–West CPC troughs with specular walls (RIR 0.98, Rsol 0.97). It traces 16,000 emission bundles from the emitter and also from the mirrors, which radiate through their 2 % emissivity. Reciprocity gives the net exchange per unit roof area. The tracer conserves energy to machine precision with ideal mirrors, and the flat cell reproduces the analytic result to 0.1 %.

Net IR per roof area vs. ΔT

Funnels win only in the deep sub-ambient corner: +50 % at −15 K with a broadband emitter in dry air, and 4.8× the flat value at −10 K in humid air. With a selective emitter, cavities gain only in humid air at −15 K and below. Everywhere else the flat plate leads or is within 1 W m⁻².

Aperture angular emittance

The 50° funnel cuts off sharply. The 2-D trough leaks along its axis, toward the East and West horizons.

Sun absorbed on 21 June, Wh m⁻² day⁻¹

Every mirror bounce absorbs 3 %. Funnels absorb about twice the sun of a flat PDRC sheet. That is the main reason H2 fails in daytime.

CAE-6 · system design day

Whole roof systems, water loop in the loop

Nine roof systems were run through the design day. Each counts IR (from CAE-3 optics), absorbed sun (Monte-Carlo, hourly sun path), convection (open: h = 2.8 + 3v at 2.5 m s⁻¹; under a 12 µm LDPE screen: 2.5 W m⁻²K⁻¹ with τIR 0.85) and screen re-emission. A controller circulates water only when the plate is a net sink. The result is daily heat moved to the sky as a function of water temperature.

Daily rejection vs. loop temperature

The rib grid at 85° (final design) comes within 3 % of a perfectly flat selective sheet, and it is the only one of the two that can carry the wind-screen.

Hourly net cooling, hot-dry, Tw = 20 °C

Every system is a heat gain at noon, so the plate charges a buried store at night (05:00 peak) and idles by day. An open roof also loses most of its night to convection. The screen recovers it.

CAE-7 · macro curvature

How much camber can the plate take?

A spherical-cap plate over a fixed circular plot, emitting from its top surface only. Camber adds area, but that area faces the warmer oblique sky. Up to a rise/span of 0.06 the loss is under 4 % in every case, which buys shell action and gutter falls for free. Steep domes collapse below ambient in humid air.

CAE-4 · thermal FEA

Feeding a 10 K budget

A 2-D finite-volume conduction model of the emitter sheet runs from a roll-bond channel (hw = 800 W m⁻²K⁻¹) out to the emitter, which is linearised at hᵣ = 6 W m⁻²K⁻¹ about the water temperature. Flux falls to zero at about 10 K, so any conduction drop comes straight off the output. Aluminium roll-bond at one channel per 150 mm reaches 94 %. Printed UHPC needs a channel under every cell row, and polymer sheet is not viable.

Emitter efficiency η = Q / Q(Temitter = Tw).

CAE-5 · structure

A plate that is mostly sky

Load path: 3 × 3 m sandwich tiles span to purlins at 3 m. The purlins span 6 m to a two-way glulam grillage. The grillage nodes are the branch tips of steel tree columns at 12 m centres. With four diagonal branches reaching (±3, ±3) m, the tips of neighbouring trees form a uniform 6 m grid. Partial factors 1.35 G + 1.5 Q, with deflection limit L/200 for tiles and L/300 for beams.

CAE-8 · insulating covers

The best thick insulation is air the plate keeps still

Covers compared on the CAE-6 design days with the selective emitter. The first is a 12 µm film over a still-air gap of 10–150 mm held by vertical mirror ribs, with the ribs ray-traced. The second is polyethylene-aerogel slabs of 3–20 mm (literature values: 6 mm reflects 92 % of sun and transmits 80 % in the window, with k = 28 mW m⁻¹K⁻¹). A slab's path length grows as 1/cos θ, so it also dims the warm horizon. Even so, it removes more cold sky than it saves in conduction. Aerogel conducts like still air, and air under a cold plate cannot convect.

Gains flatten beyond about 100 mm. The dashed line doubles the gap's heat transfer to stand in for wind-driven film flutter. Even then, a 50 mm gap matches an ideal 26 mm one.

CAE-9 · evaporation and airflow

Which sinks are actually colder than the loop?

Each line is the coldest temperature a sink can deliver in each month, compared with the 16–21 °C radiant loop. The sky line is the night stagnation temperature of the round-2 plate. Wet-bulb comes from monthly dew points. Soil temperatures use the Kusuda model, shaded by the plate. Clouds follow a simple Martin–Berdahl correction applied to the Berdahl–Martin clear-sky emissivity.

Sink temperatures by month

In humid summer only the sky line drops below the loop band. In the hot-dry monsoon (July–August) the sky still sits about 10 K below the band, while the night wet-bulb lies inside it, too close to the loop for a cooling tower.

Evaporation costs about 1.8 L of water per kWh, including blowdown. A chiller on a thermoelectric grid consumes roughly 0.5 L per kWh of cooling upstream (approximate US average). So in a water-scarce desert, evaporation is a peak tool, not a base-load one.

CAE-10 · deep ground and thick domes

Thick is time, insulating is resistance

A transient radial finite-volume model of a buried, well-mixed water pit cycled between 12 and 20 °C against soil at its 10 m temperature, run for six years. The soil shell around the pit also stores cold, so effective losses run 1.5–1.9× the steady-state shape factor. They still fall fast with size, because area grows as V2/3.

Annual soil heat gain ÷ pit capacity

Seasonal cold storage only pays above a few thousand m³. The arid site's warmer soil (26.4 °C) costs the most.

Daily-cycle response of a "thick dome"

Decrement is the fraction of the outdoor daily swing that reaches the inside. Earth stores, foam resists, and the vault uses both. Under the plate the vault's outer surface sees the shaded air mean, not the sun.

CAE-11 · year-round integration

How many storeys can one plate carry?

Daily steps over three years on monthly climate normals. Loads are internal gains of 0.20 kWh m⁻² day⁻¹, the envelope to air or soil at depth, and 0.8 L s⁻¹ m⁻² of fresh air with 75 % heat recovery. Dehumidification is optional. The plate cools return water at 19.5 °C directly and charges the pit at 15 °C with any spare hours. The pit loses heat to the soil as found in CAE-10. Evaporative trim runs only when wet-bulb + 3 K is below 19.5 °C.

Construction

A roof made on coil lines, not on site

Coil-to-tile skin line

Roll-bonded channel sheet is coil-coated with the selective emitter, roll-to-roll. Ribs are stamped from a second coil and laser-welded at the cell corners. The PIR core and soffit are laminated on a continuous sandwich press. The LDPE screen is heat-sealed to the rib tops under slight tension. The result is a 3 × 3 m tile of about 10 kg m⁻² (15 assumed in design), plumbed by two push-fit manifold stubs.

Doubly-curved from flat tiles

The product-parabola camber (rise 1.9 m over 48 m) has a minimum radius of about 150 m. A 3 m tile deviates from the surface by at most 8 mm along an edge, so flat tiles sit on purlin seatings cut to the surface. The grillage is CNC-cut from the same surface definition the CAD exports. The geometry costs nothing to build and gives drainage to all four edges.

Dig once, build down

Secant-pile walls go in first and enclose both the sunk storeys and the pit below them. The work then proceeds top-down: the vault is cast on the ground before excavating beneath it, which avoids temporary propping. Excavation is about 35,000 m³ for three storeys and a 6,000 m³ pit. Up to about 2,000 m³ of the spoil returns as vault cover and rammed earth. The tree columns continue down as the sunk building's columns and bear on the pit roof slab.

Variants for other sites

Where the water table is high, the pit becomes an aquifer store, and the sunk storeys drop to one or two. In humid climates a funnel skin (55–65°, broadband emitter) is still the only skin positive at 10 K below ambient, but the deep still-air cell wins over the year. There, cavities are the only skin that stays net-positive at 10 K below ambient (CAE-3). For a low-cost build, 3-D-printed UHPC tiles with a printed duct under every cell row reach η 0.77 (CAE-4).

Limits of this study

What would change the answer

  • The sky model is a clear-sky, two-band Beer–Lambert model. Cloud cover removes the window, which a MODTRAN run per site would capture.
  • Convection is lumped, and the film's own temperature is set at Ta − 2 K. CFD of the screened cell is the next step.
  • The massing study treats an isolated building. In a dense city, neighbours fill the horizon, which strengthens H1.
  • Structural checks are hand-calc level. A shell FE model of the cambered grillage, with wind tunnel or CFD pressure coefficients for the open canopy, comes next.
  • Dew and dust on the film (soiling) are not modelled. Expect 5–15 % loss without washing.
  • The annual model uses rounded monthly climate normals and a synthetic average day, not hourly typical-year weather. Heatwaves and cloudy runs are therefore smoothed out. Size the pit with a 1.5× margin.
  • Groundwater, excavation cost and cut-and-fill are flagged but not priced. Pit stratification is idealised as a 12–20 °C swing.
  • The aerogel properties come from published lab samples. Thicker or more transparent aerogels would shift CAE-8 but cannot remove the 1/cos θ path-length penalty.
Design rule

Floors per plot

The sky cooling a site can capture is capped by its plan area. In round 1, with night-only operation and a daily tank, that allowed one to two storeys. Storage across seasons changes the rule. The cap becomes the plate's annual capacity minus pit losses, not its worst summer week, and the hot-dry site then carries four storeys. Above ambient the rule flips: when rejecting process heat at +15 K, tall shapes buy land efficiency at the cost of skin.

Nfloors ≲ (Σyear Qplate − Qpit loss) / (Σyear qload · Astorey) when the pit ≥ the summer deficit
